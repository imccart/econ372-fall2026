Class 22 — When hospitals merge

Thursday, November 12

This recap comes from a recording of class, transcribed with Whisper and summarized with Claude, with a light edit from me. It’s meant as a convenience rather than a careful document, so the phrasing will sometimes be a little off. If something here looks wrong, please tell me and I’ll fix it.

What we covered

How we measure whether a market is competitive, what that measure does and doesn’t tell you, and the problem you have to solve before you can use it at all, which is deciding what counts as the market. Then the trends in hospital concentration, the policies behind them, and the three kinds of merger.

Key points

  • The HHI is the sum of squared market shares, and the squaring is what separates five equal firms from one firm with nearly everything. One thing to watch is the scale. Shares as decimals give an index that runs up to 1, shares as whole percentages give one that runs up to 10,000, and you’ll see both. The thresholds are conventions rather than theory, and they move. The slides use the 2010 guidelines’ lines, above 2,500 highly concentrated and below 1,500 unconcentrated, and the agencies’ 2023 guidelines lowered those to 1,800 and 1,000. The DOJ and FTC see far more proposed mergers than they can investigate, so they need a filter for which ones get a closer look. Concentrated means less competitive, so the two words run in opposite directions.
  • There is a theory behind the number. With firms choosing quantities and the price depending on total output, each firm’s markup over marginal cost as a share of price is its market share divided by the market elasticity of demand. Average that across firms, weighting by share, and the sum of squared shares is what’s left, so the HHI is the average markup in the market scaled by the elasticity. I won’t ask you to reproduce the derivation. The result does rest on strong assumptions, homogeneous products among them, and in practice people use the HHI as a descriptive statistic, the way you’d use a mean, without the theory attached.
  • Defining the market is where the argument actually happens, and it’s easier to see outside health care. Suppose you’re advising Kroger and Albertsons and want the combined firm to look small. You list every competitor you can, Walmart, Publix, Costco, Sam’s, Dollar General, the gas station that sells food, Amazon, and your share is tiny. Now switch sides and argue it’s large. Costco sells everything, so it isn’t a grocery competitor, and Whole Foods and Trader Joe’s are a different product. Narrow the set and the denominator shrinks, so the same firm’s share grows with nothing about the firms changed. For a hospital this happens on two dimensions at once. In product space, I can argue that a merger with an orthopedic hospital changes nothing because I don’t do orthopedics. In geographic space, Emory’s own zip code gives it a hundred percent share, which is absurd because patients come from everywhere, but every wider boundary has a version of the same problem, and the right one depends on the service, since the market for a gunshot wound is much smaller than the market for a transplant. The two defensible starting points are the hospital referral region, which you’ve seen, and patient flows, where you walk out zip code by zip code, keep the ones whose patients mostly come to you, and stop where they mostly go somewhere else.
  • Hospital markets have grown more concentrated for decades, however you define them, and mergers and closures both push the same way. On the metro-area tool we looked at, Johnson City, Tennessee is essentially one system, Atlanta sits somewhere in the middle, and only a handful of metro areas fall below the 2,500 line at all. Being concentrated brings no regulation by itself. It matters when a merger is proposed. The connection to the bargaining model is that a hospital’s outside option in a negotiation is its market power, so thirty years of rising concentration is the same thing as thirty years of rising prices.
  • Why it happened is mostly policy. For a long time the view was that competition among hospitals is wasteful. If you’re a city paying for a hospital, a second one nearby means paying for a second set of MRI machines to do the same work, and if you believe any bed you build will get filled, which is the supplier-induced demand argument in its bluntest form, then you limit beds to limit spending. The same logic applies to Medicare and Medicaid as payers. That produced certificate-of-need laws, which require a would-be hospital to show the community needs it before it can open, and many are still on the books. It’s a barrier to entry and it works. If we want a market to allocate health care, though, we need hospitals to open and compete, and for decades policy pushed the other way. The evidence doesn’t support the quality defense of consolidation either. Mergers and closures don’t improve care, and if anything quality slips a little, mostly on the patient-experience side, while prices go up reliably. Entry is so rare in this industry that we mostly learn about competition by watching competitors disappear.
  • A certificate of public advantage, which is what this week’s discussion forum is about, lets a state approve a merger that would otherwise face federal antitrust review, on the condition that the state supervises the merged system itself. The problem comes at the end. The certificate expires, the oversight goes with it, and the merger doesn’t come undone.
  • Within-market mergers are the well-understood case, and the price effects are large, twenty to forty percent in many studies and up to sixty in some, a $15,000 hip replacement becoming more than $20,000. The effect is bigger the closer the two hospitals are, for the bargaining reason again. If an insurer can send patients next door when it drops me, I’m weak, and once next door is mine the nearest alternative is far away. Prices at hospitals that weren’t part of the merger rise too, since the whole market is more concentrated. These mergers are getting rarer mainly because systems are running out of local competitors to buy.
  • Out-of-market mergers are now the common kind, with smaller but real price effects of five to ten percent, and they’re harder to challenge because the mechanism isn’t obvious. If Emory buys a hospital in Tennessee, nothing about your choices in Atlanta changed. There are two explanations. Common customers, since hospital markets are local but insurance markets aren’t, so the employer and the insurer span both places even if no patient does. And multi-market contact, or mutual forbearance, an idea that comes from airlines. If Delta is strong in Atlanta and American is strong in Dallas and the two meet on many other routes, neither starts a price war in the other’s stronghold, because the response would come on every shared route, and both end up pricing higher where they’re strong.
  • Vertical deals, hospitals buying physician practices and insurers buying hospitals and nursing facilities, go almost unchallenged because there isn’t a model a court can apply. Prices still rise, through the same outside-option logic, since physicians I own don’t refer outside my system. The usual defense of these acquisitions is that they rescue struggling hospitals. In the data, most mergers are between two large, healthy systems.

One follow-up

I said in class I’d check what happened with Kroger and Albertsons. Courts blocked the merger in December 2024, Albertsons walked away and sued Kroger for the $600 million termination fee and a good deal more, and that trial just finished in Delaware with no ruling expected until sometime next year.
