Class 18 — How hospitals set prices

Thursday, October 29

This recap comes from a recording of class, transcribed with Whisper and summarized with Claude, with a light edit from me. It’s meant as a convenience rather than a careful document, so the phrasing will sometimes be a little off. If something here looks wrong, please tell me and I’ll fix it.

What we covered

We finished the two slides left over from the hospital-price class, price transparency and competition, and then started on the pricing itself. A hospital with price-setting power, a hospital that simply takes the price it’s given, and the beginning of the case where it faces both at once. The first fifteen minutes went to your questions about the exam.

Key points

  • Price transparency sounds obvious and isn’t. Start with what you’d actually have to post. A vet can put a chart on the wall because the service is known in advance and the price is the same for everyone. Walk into an emergency department and neither holds. Nobody knows yet which of twenty things is wrong with you, finding out is itself most of what gets billed, and the price depends on who your insurer is. Even if we solved all of that, acting on the number is a separate problem. You don’t know what treatment you need, a lot of care isn’t planned, your network restricts where you can go, and a cheaper hospital you have to reach by ambulance isn’t cheaper. Transparency can also push the wrong way for some people. If the lowest posted price is already past what you can pay and you’re uninsured, the number deters you from going at all. Forgone care is real.
  • Posted prices can raise prices, and whether they do depends on who gets to see them. If prices are visible to patients only, and patients respond, you get the story you’d expect, competition for the cheaper option. If they’re visible to the whole market, I can walk into a negotiation and say you’re paying the hospital across town more than you’re paying me for the same service, so pay me that. Nothing there requires coordination. It follows straight from the bargaining model. What turns it off is a patient who actually uses the information, and we have good reasons to doubt that, since insurance absolves you of most of the difference, the tools are hard to use, and much of the decision isn’t yours anyway. Economists said this before the rule existed rather than after, and the clearest precedent, posted prices in the Danish concrete market, saw prices rise. How the rule was written then mattered as much as that it passed. Hospitals have to post in both a machine-readable and a consumer-friendly format, and what they produced was thousands of unlabeled codes spread across spreadsheets and JSON files, with some posting nothing at all for years before any fine arrived. Compliance is better now and still incomplete, and the quality is poor enough that an industry exists to scrape these files, clean them, and sell the result back. A disclosure rule is only as good as the format it specifies and the enforcement behind it.
  • More competition usually lowers prices, but not when firms compete on more than price. If patients are very responsive to quality and barely responsive to price, then competing means competing on quality, and quality has to be paid for, so more competition can raise the price. That isn’t a reason to think competition doesn’t matter here. We allocate health care through a market and then don’t supply the market with much competition, which is the worst of the two arrangements. The Fort Wayne reading is the case study. One system bought up the hospitals and physician practices around it and raised prices continuously, with no competitor to stop it and insurers who couldn’t walk away from the only hospital in town.
  • A hospital with price-setting power is the firm you already know, and we don’t need a separate model for not-for-profits. You can write one down, utility over profit from private insurers, profit from public payers, and the quantity of care delivered, but you can’t solve it without knowing how much weight sits on each piece, so it predicts nothing. We assume profit maximization for everyone instead. Then it’s marginal revenue equals marginal cost, and price up to the demand curve. With inverse demand \(p = 16 - q\) and costs \(c(q) = 5 + q^{2}\), write profit as \((16-q)q - 5 - q^{2}\), differentiate to get \(16 - 4q = 0\), so \(q = 4\), \(p = 12\), marginal cost is 8, and the markup is 4, or fifty percent. Work from the profit function rather than memorizing marginal revenue. You won’t always be able to see what the marginal revenue function is, and the derivative never changes. Two things to carry. Marginal revenue falls faster than demand because selling one more unit means dropping the price for everyone, not just the marginal buyer, and if you could price-discriminate perfectly the two would be the same line. And a pure price taker has a flat marginal revenue curve, which is the whole model: marginal revenue is the money you get for the next unit of care, an outside payer has fixed that number, so only your cost curve shapes the decision. That is not capitation. Under capitation the revenue is fixed in total and each additional unit of care reduces your profit.
  • Put the two together and you get a kinked marginal revenue curve, which is the one new idea. Picture two lines of patients at the door, one commercial and one Medicare, and take them in order of marginal revenue. The commercial patients start above the Medicare price, so you serve them first, and at the point where commercial marginal revenue has fallen to the Medicare price you switch lines. Marginal revenue slopes down and then goes flat. Total quantity is still where marginal revenue meets marginal cost, you just have to know which branch you’re standing on, which makes this a logic problem on top of a derivative problem. The private quantity is where the two marginal revenue curves meet, the gap up to the total is the public quantity, and the private price comes from pricing up to demand at the private quantity, not at the total. Now cut the Medicare price. The kink moves right, the private quantity rises, and the private price falls. The reason is clearer in patients than in curves. A Medicare patient just became less valuable, you’d rather fill that slot with a commercial patient, and the only way to attract one more commercial patient is to charge less. That is the opposite of cost shifting, which is the hospital industry’s claim that cutting Medicare rates forces them to raise commercial prices to make up the difference. Keep cost shifting separate from cross-subsidization, which is funding a money-losing service out of a profitable one, happens routinely, and isn’t controversial.

Worth knowing for the exam

The exam is all short answer, no multiple choice. Every question comes in four or five parts, and a couple of those parts will be a number, maybe a graph, and then a short discussion of what you found. The setup will look like what we write on the board. Here’s a benefit function, here’s a constraint, here’s an outside option, now find the optimum.

The difficulty is the difficulty of the in-class problems and the worksheets on the website. Work through all of them. Any review I hand out is a version of those with the numbers changed, a utility function to the one-third instead of a square root. Nothing new will appear.

There’s more on you this term than there used to be, and it’s worth knowing why. The homework used to carry theory questions as well as empirical ones, and take-home theory stopped being useful once everyone could get a perfect answer without doing it. So the homework is empirical now, and the quizzes are too short to hold a real derivation, which means nothing has forced you to practice the computations. The material is all covered. The practice is yours to go get.

Come talk to me or the TA, and come with something specific. I worked this problem, I got this answer, I don’t see where it went wrong. That’s a conversation. What’s on the exam isn’t.

On grading, roughly three points a question, zero for blank or for restating the question, one point for a real start that doesn’t get there. Partial credit is discrete, so don’t expect fractions of a point. It’s on paper, one part per page, and you won’t need all the pages. If you find yourself writing an essay, you’ve gone wrong. Explain the intuition of your answer, not the mechanics of taking a derivative. You can bring your own formula sheet.
