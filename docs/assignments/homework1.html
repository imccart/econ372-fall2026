Homework 1 — Adverse Selection in Real Markets

Instructions

This homework is about health insurance markets and adverse selection, the material from the first module of the course. It is empirical only — the theory is assessed in class through the quizzes and the midterm. Here you work with real data and interpret it as a health economist.

You are expected to use an AI coding assistant. Use GitHub Copilot (free with your Emory GitHub account) or the tool of your choice, and let it write the R or Python. Producing the code is not the skill being graded. Directing the tool, checking that the output makes sense, and interpreting the result in the language of this course is. Read what the assistant gives you, make sure it actually answers the question, and explain what it means.

Submit a rendered notebook (Quarto, R Markdown, or Jupyter) containing your code, its output, and your written answers. Each part is worth 3 points:

  • 3 points: correct, with sound economic reasoning.
  • 2 points: close, with a minor error or thin reasoning.
  • 1 point: attempted, but the economic reasoning is missing or wrong.
  • 0 points: no real work, or output pasted in with no interpretation.

Homework 1 is due by midnight on Friday, September 25.

Q1 — New Jersey’s individual market, 2000–2008 (15 pts)

Data: nj-insurance.csv (on the shared OneDrive, linked from Canvas). It has monthly family premiums and year-end enrollment for three plans in New Jersey’s Individual Health Coverage program: Horizon BCBS Plan D ($1,000 deductible), Aetna Life Plan D ($1,000 deductible), and Celtic Insurance Plan C ($2,500 deductible).

  1. Have the assistant build a table of annual premiums for the three plans and total enrollment, and plot premiums and enrollment over time.

  2. This pattern, premiums climbing while enrollment falls, is often called a “death spiral.” Using the adverse-selection model from class, explain the mechanism behind it: when premiums rise, which enrollees are most likely to drop their coverage, and how does the changing mix of who remains push premiums up again?

  3. Two of these plans carry a $1,000 deductible and one carries $2,500, and you are comparing premiums in nominal dollars across 2000–2008. Explain how each of these makes a raw premium comparison misleading, and correct at least one of them (for example, adjust for inflation, or compare only the like-deductible plans). Show what changes.

  4. Adverse selection is not the only reason premiums might rise over this period. Name one other force we discussed in class, such as the general growth in health care costs, and explain how it could produce the same rising-premium pattern without any change in who is enrolled.

  5. Give your bottom-line read: does this look like an adverse-selection death spiral, premium growth from rising costs, or some of both? Defend your answer in a few sentences using what you found in the data.

Q2 — Medicare Advantage and fee-for-service, 2008–2023 (15 pts)

Data: two county-year files (same place on the shared OneDrive). medicare-ma-penetration.csv gives, for each county and year, the total number of Medicare eligibles and the number enrolled in Medicare Advantage (MA). medicare-ffs-costs.csv gives, for each county and year, fee-for-service (FFS) enrollment and total FFS spending.

  1. Merge the two files on county and year, and roll the result up to national totals by year. Then compute two national series: MA penetration (MA enrolled divided by total eligibles) and FFS spending per enrollee (total FFS spending divided by FFS enrollment). Plot both over time. To get a national rate, sum the counts across counties first and then take the ratio, rather than averaging the county rates, and explain in a sentence why those two approaches give different answers.

  2. As MA penetration rises, people are moving out of fee-for-service and into MA. Using the idea of selection from class, explain who tends to choose MA and who tends to stay in FFS, and what that does to the average health, and the average cost, of the people who remain in FFS.

  3. Total FFS spending and FFS spending per enrollee can move in different directions. If MA enrollment grows, what happens to total FFS spending purely mechanically, even when the cost of caring for each remaining person has not changed? Using both series you computed, explain which one actually tells you something about selection and which mostly reflects how many people are left in FFS.

  4. Medicare pays MA plans more for enrollees who are expected to be sicker, which is the risk adjustment we covered in class. In plain terms, what problem is risk adjustment meant to solve, and how does it change a plan’s incentive to attract healthy versus sick enrollees?

  5. Name one other feature of the Medicare Advantage market from class, such as the extra benefits plans can offer or the way plans are marketed, that could affect who signs up, and explain how.
